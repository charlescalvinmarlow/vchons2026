Charles Marlow is a Sydney/Eora based designer interested in exploring digital technologies, their infrastructures, and the impact they have on how we understand the world. Working to communicate ideas through animation, web, code, and exhibition practices, he invites audiences into the conversation, and attempts to get them to further consider their own relationships to technology.

Project Synopsis

What if behind the scenes, the Internet was a forest of mushrooms? Would it make any difference to us, as users? The Internet's infrastructures and use of resources are so hidden from view that it becomes almost impossible to even imagine.

Forest of the.Cloud is a large-scale digital installation where you are invited to explore a dark forest, populated by mushrooms representing the computers that pass your data across the vast dark network of the Internet. At the start of the experience, a QR code lets a phone connect to the server, and then you can draw your own mushroom and move a light through the forest with your phone.

Developed as a website using the HTML, CSS and JavaScript languages of the web, I worked across web design, experience design, code, prose and illustration. The work was developed in the UTS Data Arena, a 360º panoramic multi-user interactive theatre, but as it is a website, it can be displayed on a smaller projection or screens.

I also wanted to challenge the large amounts of data expected in a digital project, and experimented to build the experience to be 267KB uncompressed (the equivalent file size of around one or two images on Instagram). This is achieved by using code to build large images out of smaller tiny images, and by using CSS where possible to create elements such as the light controlled by the phone. The files are then hosted on a small computer positioned at the end of the experience, to display the ephemeral ‘server’, an aspect of the Internet you rarely get to see.

Through all of this, I hope to provide audiences with a new perspective on their relationship to the Internet, and to remind them that despite what we are told, none of this comes for free.
